Homes built decades ago near Littleton's historic downtown core and the original townsite grid have had far more time to settle than newer construction further out. That settling shows up as small cracks and gaps in the foundation, exactly the kind a mouse or rat is capable of using.

Why older foundations develop more gaps.

Foundations shift gradually as soil beneath them settles, especially through repeated freeze-thaw cycles typical of a Colorado winter. A foundation poured seventy or eighty years ago has been through far more of these cycles than a foundation poured in the last decade, and each cycle has the potential to widen an existing hairline crack or open a new one.

Older homes also tend to have had more renovation work done over the years, new HVAC systems, updated plumbing, added electrical circuits, each one requiring a new penetration through the foundation that may or may not have been properly sealed at the time.

Where to look first.

  • Foundation corners, where settling stress concentrates.
  • Around any visible utility penetration, old or new.
  • Where the foundation meets the sill plate.
  • Any area with visible mortar deterioration on masonry foundations.
A hairline crack this year is often an entry point by next winter.

Materials matched to the gap.

Caulk alone does not hold up against a determined mouse, which can chew through it within days. Smaller gaps around pipes and wiring get packed with steel wool before sealing. Larger openings, vents and bigger cracks, get galvanized hardware cloth. True foundation cracks and masonry deterioration get a proper mortar repair or sheet metal patch, not a cosmetic fix.

The specific material matters less than matching it correctly to the gap. A small gap over-sealed with the wrong material can fail just as easily as a large gap under-sealed with the right one.

A realistic timeline for an older home.

A typical older Littleton home with multiple settled gaps usually takes a full visit to seal properly, longer than a newer home with just one or two specific entry points. That is not because the work itself is more complex at each individual gap, but because there are simply more of them to find and address.

Homes with prior rodent problems often show evidence of a previous owner's attempted fix, a smear of expanding foam or a scrap of mesh stuffed into a gap. That old fix needs to be removed and redone properly, rather than left in place and built around.

What a thorough seal job prevents.

Benefits of properly sealing an older foundation.

  • Entry points close for good instead of reopening after one season.
  • Energy efficiency improves alongside pest prevention.
  • Moisture intrusion through the same gaps is reduced as a side benefit.

Sealing an older foundation properly tends to pay off in more than just rodent prevention, since the same gaps that let mice in often let drafts and moisture through as well.

Signs your foundation already has a problem gap.

Beyond a visible crack, a few other signs suggest an existing gap is already in use. Fresh dirt or debris pushed out from a crack, a slight draft near one section of basement wall even on a calm day, or a musty smell localized to one area rather than spread evenly through the basement, are all worth checking.

None of these signs are definitive on their own, but together they help prioritize which section of an older foundation to focus on first during an inspection, rather than treating the entire perimeter as equally likely to be the active entry point.

When a full foundation isn't necessary.

Not every older home needs a full-perimeter sealing job. Some houses have one or two active gaps and the rest of the foundation, despite its age, remains tight. An honest inspection identifies which gaps are actually active rather than sealing every visible crack regardless of whether it is actually being used as an entry point, which keeps the job focused and the price fair.

A note on DIY foundation sealing.

A handy homeowner can reasonably handle small, obvious gaps with steel wool and sealant. Larger foundation cracks or masonry deterioration are worth having assessed professionally, since misjudging the material or the size of a gap can mean the fix fails within a season.

A home inspector's report from when the house was purchased can also be a useful starting point, since many inspectors note foundation condition even without flagging it as a rodent concern specifically. Reviewing that report before a rodent inspection can sometimes highlight an area worth checking first.

Whichever route you take, professional or DIY, the goal is the same: a gap that actually stays closed through a full Colorado winter, not a quick fix that needs redoing again by spring.

Safe handling checklist for droppings and nests

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

Will sealing my foundation fix drafts too?

Often yes, as a side benefit, since the same gaps mice use frequently let in outside air as well.

How long does sealing an older home usually take?

A full visit is typical for an older home with multiple settled gaps, compared to a quicker visit for a newer home with just one or two specific entry points.

Is foundation sealing a one-time fix?

A properly sealed gap should hold long-term, but foundations continue to settle gradually, so periodic re-inspection, especially after any new renovation work, is worth considering.
