Choosing a rodent control company is harder than choosing most home services, since the actual quality of the work, whether entry points were really found and sealed, is not something you can easily verify yourself afterward. A handful of direct questions before booking can reveal a lot about how a company actually operates.

Does the price include an inspection, or just a guess?

Ask directly whether the quoted price is based on an actual inspection or a generic phone estimate. A company that insists on seeing the property before finalizing a price is generally giving you a more accurate number than one willing to quote a flat rate sight unseen, since the real scope of a rodent job varies enormously from house to house.

Does the plan include sealing, or just trapping?

Trapping without sealing often means a temporary fix. Ask specifically whether entry point sealing is part of the recommended plan, and if so, what materials are used. Steel wool, hardware cloth and sheet metal hold up; caulk alone generally does not against a determined mouse.

  • Is the inspection included in the price, or billed separately?
  • What materials are used for sealing entry points?
  • Is there a follow-up visit included, or is it a single visit only?
  • How is chewed wiring, insulation or duct damage priced?
A company that cannot explain its own process clearly is not a company that will explain your invoice clearly either.

Is damage repair priced separately and documented?

Chewed insulation, ducts or wiring found during an inspection should be documented, ideally with photos, and priced as its own line item rather than folded quietly into a larger total. Ask how the company handles damage specifically, since the answer reveals a lot about how transparent their pricing really is.

Is there a follow-up visit included?

A single visit rarely resolves an active infestation completely. Ask whether a follow-up to check traps and confirm sealing is holding is included in the price or billed as an additional visit, so there are no surprises after the first appointment.

How quickly can they actually respond?

If the situation feels urgent, ask directly about same-day availability rather than assuming it based on marketing language like '24/7' alone. A company that is honest about scheduling constraints, rather than over-promising, is generally more trustworthy than one that tells you exactly what you want to hear.

Do they know the local area?

A company familiar with your area should be able to speak to.

  • Common construction types and ages in your specific neighborhood.
  • Seasonal patterns typical for your region.
  • Local landmarks or features, a park, a river, a shopping corridor, relevant to outdoor activity.

A company that can speak specifically to your area's housing stock and seasonal patterns, rather than giving generic answers that could apply anywhere, usually has more actual local experience behind the answer.

Questions specific to your situation.

Beyond the general questions above, it helps to ask something specific to your actual situation: if you have pets, ask how bait placement accounts for them. If you have a finished basement, ask how they plan to inspect behind finished walls without unnecessary demolition. A company that answers specific questions thoughtfully, rather than falling back on a generic script, is usually one that has handled a wide range of real situations rather than a narrow set of easy ones.

What a good answer sounds like.

A good answer to most of these questions acknowledges uncertainty honestly where it exists. 'We will not know the full scope until we inspect, but here is roughly what a job like this usually involves' is a more trustworthy answer than a confident number given before anyone has seen the property. The goal of asking these questions is not to catch anyone in a lie, but to get a feel for how transparent the company is likely to be once you are already a customer.

Trust your own follow-up questions.

If an answer to any of these questions leaves you more confused rather than more informed, that itself is useful information. A company confident in its own process should be able to explain it in plain terms, without resorting to vague reassurances or jargon that does not actually answer what was asked.

It is also reasonable to ask how a company handles a situation where the first visit does not fully resolve things. A company with a clear answer, a defined follow-up process rather than a vague promise to circle back, is one that has clearly thought through what happens when a job needs more than a single visit.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Sources and standards

Public guidance behind the advice on this page. It describes general practice and rules, not a claim about any one provider.

  • CDC: How to Clean Up After Rodents: The public-health steps for airing out a space, wetting droppings with disinfectant instead of sweeping, and bagging waste.

Common questions

Should I be suspicious of a very low quoted price?

A price given before any inspection should be treated as preliminary at best. The real cost depends on what the inspection finds, so a very low number quoted sight unseen is worth questioning.

Is it reasonable to ask for references or reviews?

It is reasonable to ask, though we focus our own answers here on our actual process and pricing rather than claims we cannot independently verify for you.

What if a company refuses to explain their pricing clearly?

That is worth treating as a red flag. A company confident in its process should be able to explain inspection, treatment, sealing and follow-up pricing clearly before you commit.
