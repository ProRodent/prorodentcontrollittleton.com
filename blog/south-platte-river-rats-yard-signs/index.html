Homes with a yard backing onto the South Platte River greenbelt, or close to one of the irrigation ditches that run through parts of Littleton, tend to see more outdoor Norway rat activity than properties set further back on an interior street. Water, cover and food access close together are exactly what this species looks for.

Why water-adjacent yards matter so much.

Norway rats are strong swimmers and comfortable around water in a way house mice generally are not, which is part of why riverside and ditch-adjacent properties see a disproportionate share of outdoor rat calls compared to mouse calls. The combination of water, mature tree cover and typically looser, more easily burrowed soil near a riverbank gives rats most of what they need within a short distance.

Irrigation ditches specifically offer a reliable water source much of the growing season, along with dense vegetation along the banks that provides cover from predators and weather.

Yard signs to watch for near the river.

  • Burrow openings along the riverbank, ditch bank, or rear fence line.
  • Rub marks, greasy smears, on fence posts or low branches along a regular route.
  • Gnaw marks on irrigation equipment, hoses or wooden garden structures.
  • Droppings near compost piles, bird feeders or stored firewood.
A rat burrow near water rarely has just one entrance, and rarely stays empty for long if left untreated.

Food sources that compound the problem.

Compost piles, bird feeders and loosely secured trash cans are common in yards near green space, and all three give rats an added food source on top of what the river or ditch environment already provides. A property that addresses the water-adjacent cover but leaves an open compost pile nearby often sees activity return regardless.

Fruit trees, if present, add yet another seasonal food source, since fallen fruit left on the ground is an easy, reliable meal for a rat already established nearby.

How treatment is adjusted for riverside properties.

Backyard and outdoor rodent control for a riverside or ditch-adjacent property spends more time on burrow identification and treatment along the water-facing side of the yard than a typical interior-block visit would. Burrows get treated at the entrance, addressing the full system rather than just the visible opening, since a burrow near water often connects to more than one exit.

Once outdoor activity near the water is addressed, the house itself still gets checked for any gap a yard-dwelling rat could use to move indoors as the weather changes, since riverside properties are not exempt from that risk just because the primary activity is outdoors.

Practical steps for riverside yards.

If your yard backs onto the river or a ditch.

  • Keep compost bins secured and away from the water-facing fence line.
  • Pick up fallen fruit promptly if you have fruit trees.
  • Trim vegetation back from the immediate riverbank or ditch edge where practical.
  • Mention the water-adjacent location when you call, since it changes what we check first.

None of these steps eliminate rat activity near a water source entirely, since the river and its ecosystem are not going anywhere. They do reduce how attractive a specific yard is relative to the general area, which is often enough to meaningfully cut down on activity.

Seasonal patterns along the river.

Activity near the South Platte greenbelt follows its own seasonal rhythm, somewhat different from strictly residential blocks. Warmer months bring more visible burrow digging and vegetation damage, while cooler months see rats relying more on established burrows and moving toward nearby structures as outdoor food becomes scarcer. A yard inspection in late summer or early fall can catch a developing burrow system before it becomes a larger problem over winter.

Spring flooding or heavy snowmelt along the river can also temporarily displace burrow systems, sometimes pushing activity toward higher ground, which for some properties means closer to the house itself for a period each year.

Working with what the landscape gives you.

Nobody can fully eliminate the South Platte River or its greenbelt as a factor. Most homeowners near it do not want to, since the same green space that supports some rodent activity is also why many people choose to live there. The realistic goal is managing the risk at the yard's edge, not removing it entirely.

A note for renters near the greenbelt.

If you rent a property near the South Platte greenbelt, outdoor rodent signs are still worth reporting to a landlord or property manager promptly, since yard-level activity left unaddressed tends to move toward the structure over time, same as it would for an owner-occupied home.

Safe handling checklist for droppings and nests

General safety practice drawn from CDC guidance on rodents. It is meant to keep you safe before any professional arrives.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Sources and standards

Public guidance behind the advice on this page. It describes general practice and rules, not a claim about any one provider.

Common questions

Does living near the South Platte River mean I will definitely have rats?

Not definitely, but properties directly adjacent to the river or an irrigation ditch do see more outdoor Norway rat activity on average than interior-block properties, mainly from the combination of water, cover and food access.

Will trimming vegetation along the riverbank actually help?

It can reduce the cover available right at the yard's edge, though it will not eliminate activity along a shared public greenbelt entirely. It is one practical step among several.

Are irrigation ditches treated differently from the main river?

The same general risk factors, water, cover, food, apply to both, though a narrower ditch may have less overall habitat than the river's wider greenbelt.
