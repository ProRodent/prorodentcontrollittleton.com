Why Ketring Park needs rodent control

  • Lakefront cover and mature trees

    Ketring Lake's landscaped edges and the mature trees around the park give rodents cover close to nearby homes, more than a typical inland residential block would see.

  • Homes backing onto the park

    Properties with a rear yard facing the lake or the park's open lawns have a different outdoor pressure point than homes set further back on an interior street.

  • Seasonal foot traffic and food left behind

    The park draws walkers, picnickers and wildlife watchers through much of the year, and food left behind, intentionally or not, adds to what is already available near the water.

Centered on Ketring Lake

Ketring Park and the neighborhood around it take their character from Ketring Lake itself, a small community lake surrounded by lawns, mature trees and walking paths. That combination of water, tree cover and steady foot traffic gives this part of Littleton a different rodent pressure profile than neighborhoods without a comparable green space nearby.

Water and tree cover close to homes.

Homes near Ketring Lake sit close to a combination of water, mature trees and open lawn that gives rodents, particularly Norway rats, cover and food access that a purely residential block set away from the park would not have. An inspection in this neighborhood pays particular attention to the yard side of the lot facing the park or lake, not just the house itself.

Norway rats in particular do well around lake edges with landscaped plantings, since the combination of water, cover and nearby food sources, whether from residential compost or food left behind by park visitors, gives them most of what they need within a short distance.

What draws activity near the park.

  • Lake-edge landscaping and mature tree cover checked for burrow activity.
  • Rear yards facing the park inspected for fence-line and foundation gaps.
  • Food sources, compost, feeders and picnic-area leftovers, identified and addressed.

Homes directly backing onto the park can see more outdoor burrow activity along the rear fence line than homes on the interior streets of the same neighborhood, because of the extra cover the park edge provides. That rear fence line is the first place we look.

A lake edge gives rodents exactly what an open residential block does not: water, cover, and food, all close together.

Sealing and yard work together.

For homes near Ketring Park, backyard and outdoor rodent control and standard exclusion work usually go together, since outdoor activity along the park edge is often what eventually pushes toward the house itself once the weather turns. We check both the yard and the exterior of the house on the same visit for homes in this neighborhood.

Before we arrive

  • Keep children and pets away from any area with droppings, a nest, or a suspected entry point.
  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings; that can spread contamination into the air.
  • Do not set out your own loose bait or poison before the visit.
  • Note when and where you have seen or heard activity so the technician can start there.

Questions about Ketring Park

Does living near Ketring Lake mean more rodent activity?

Homes directly backing onto the lake or park do tend to see more outdoor burrow activity than homes on interior streets, mainly from Norway rats using the lake-edge cover.

Do park visitors leaving food behind attract rodents to nearby homes?

It can contribute, along with compost and feeders on residential properties nearby. We check all of these during a yard inspection.

Should I be more concerned in a particular season?

Activity often increases as the weather cools and rodents look for cover and warmth, which is when outdoor activity near the park is most likely to move toward nearby homes.
